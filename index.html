The discount isn't the whole deal.

I analyzed a public dunnhumby grocery sales and promotion dataset covering 156 weeks, 55 products and 77 stores, to see how different promotion strategies relate to unit sales. The promotions people could actually notice showed far more lift than price cuts alone, even at the same discount.

The short version

Biggest lift

+314%

Products in the weekly ad and on display sold about four times their normal amount. A price cut alone added only 25%.

Same discount

+237% vs +29%

Even when both were about 20% off, the visible promotion still sold far more.

Deeper discounts

+43% vs +112%

A big price cut of 30% or more still did worse than a display with just 10 to 20% off.

The cost

94%

That's the margin a price cut would need just to pay for its own discount, which is far above what grocery products usually make.

Keep in mind

A pattern, not proof

Promo weeks weren't busier weeks overall, but stores may choose which products to feature, and display fees aren't in the data.

Chapter two

Same discount, very different lift

Across all promotions, price cuts averaged 21.8% off and feature and display averaged 25.2% off, so I matched them to make it a fair fight. I kept every store and product that ran both, as long as their average discounts were within 3 percentage points of each other.

A price cut on its own

+29%

  • Average discount20.5%
  • Store weeks17,522
  • Required margin to cover discount cost, all price cuts94%

In the weekly ad and on display

+237%

  • Average discount20.5%
  • Store weeks8,238
  • Required margin to cover discount cost, all feature and display38%

918 matched store and product pairs, covering 40 products in all 77 stores.

What the required margin means. Every discount gives money away, and the extra units sold have to earn it back. This is a modeled threshold showing how much gross margin a product would need for those extra units to cover the price discount. It isn't realized profit, since the data has no real product margins, and it leaves out display fees, ad costs and labor. I calculate it on pooled totals because individual rows can be unstable: 13% of price-cut store and product rows actually sold fewer units than their baseline.

Being seen is the most plausible explanation for the gap, but this data can't prove it on its own.

The recommendation flips somewhere between a 36% and 41% margin for the visible promotions, so knowing real category margins would be the first thing I'd ask for. Price cuts didn't cover their discount at any realistic margin in this sample.

Chapter seven

What I'd test next

Pair discounts with visibility, as a test

For products with margins above roughly 40%, feature and display had the strongest observed lift and covered its modeled discount cost. I'd pilot it in a set of stores against matched control stores before rolling it out anywhere.

Matched comparison at 20.5% off: +237% versus +29%.

Screen price-only events first

Before a price cut runs, I'd check its expected lift against the discount it gives away. That doesn't mean every price cut loses money, but it's where the numbers say to look first.

Bag snack price cuts in this sample would need a 175% margin to cover their discount, which fails at any conventional margin.

Spend where response is highest

I'd lean promo dollars toward the items and categories that responded most, and keep checking that the response holds up over time.

National brands showed a lift percentage 2.3 times higher than store brands on feature and display (+338% vs +148%). Frozen pizza's estimated elasticity of −2.48 (95% range −2.71 to −2.25) implies about 30% more units from a 10% price cut, versus about 13 to 14% in the other categories.

How I measured it

The data. dunnhumby's Breakfast at the Frat is a public dataset that represents sales and promotion information for four categories over 156 weeks. Each row is one product in one store for one week.

Promo types don't overlap. A week is feature and display, feature only, display only, price cut only, or no promotion.

Baseline is the average weekly units for the same store and product across its unpromoted weeks. I removed 208 rows missing a price or base price, then left out pairs with fewer than 8 unpromoted weeks (458 rows), leaving 524,284 of the original 524,950.

Lift is promoted units divided by baseline units for the same weeks, minus 1, computed on pooled totals.

Required margin to cover discount cost is discount dollars divided by incremental units at base price. It's a modeled threshold, not realized profit.

Price elasticity comes from a log-log regression of units on price within each store and product, using only weeks with no feature or display. It's observational, the 95% ranges use standard errors clustered by store and product, and a 10% price cut translates to 0.9 raised to the elasticity, minus 1.

Limits. The baseline doesn't adjust for seasonality, holidays, stock-up buying or sales pulled from other products, and promotion costs beyond the discount aren't in the data.
